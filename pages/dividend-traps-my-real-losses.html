Dividend Traps I Didn't Avoid: My Real Losses and the Warning Signs I Check Now

November 26, 2026 · By Marco Bozem, private investor · Not financial advice

I publish my portfolio, so I also publish my mistakes. Two of my holdings are textbook dividend traps, and both are high-yield stocks: Arbor Realty Trust, down about 83% against my purchase price, and Medical Properties Trust, down about 51%. Both cut the dividend. Looking back, the signs were visible before the cuts. This article writes them down, with the numbers.

My two positions as of 26 November 2026 (from my portfolio tracking)
  • Arbor Realty Trust (ABR): 108 shares, average cost €9.71, current price €1.14, unrealised result about −83%. Dividends received in 2026: €53.62 gross.
  • Medical Properties Trust (MPW): 272 shares, average cost €4.42, current price €1.68, unrealised result about −51%. Dividends received in 2026: €57.01 gross.
Together, these two positions cost me far more capital than all the dividends they ever paid me.

Case 1: Arbor Realty Trust

Arbor is a US mortgage REIT that lends mainly against multifamily properties. For years it was a favourite of dividend investors: it raised its dividend regularly until mid-2023. Then higher interest rates hit its borrowers.

The key number was the payout ratio on distributable earnings, the cash measure Arbor reports itself. When Arbor last raised the dividend in July 2023, it was paying out about 75% of distributable earnings. By early 2025 that ratio had climbed to nearly 150%. In Q1 2025 distributable earnings were USD 0.28 per share (0.31 excluding a one-off loss) against a quarterly dividend of USD 0.43. In May 2025 the board reset the dividend to USD 0.30, a cut of 30.2%.

The cut didn't end it. Over the following twelve months the share price fell from around USD 9 to about USD 1.2 by mid-November 2026, more than 80%, as earnings kept falling; in October 2026 it was dropped from S&P 600 indices. The high yield turned out to be a warning, not a gift.

What I should have seen: a payout ratio above 100% for several quarters in a row, combined with a loan book exposed to borrowers who had financed at floating rates during the low-rate years.

Case 2: Medical Properties Trust

MPT owns hospitals and leases them to operators. From 2013 to mid-2023 it raised its quarterly dividend by about a cent every year. But it had grown with a lot of debt, and some of its largest tenants ran into financial trouble.

The yield rose to around 16% before the first cut. In Q1 2023 management still spoke supportively about the dividend; a few months later the CFO said that "everything is on the table" when asked about it. In August 2023 MPT cut the quarterly dividend from USD 0.29 to USD 0.15 (−48.3%), explaining that the payout had exceeded its adjusted funds from operations. A year later it cut again, to USD 0.08.

What I should have seen: tenant concentration (a few operators producing a large share of rent), debt-funded growth, and the moment management stopped defending the dividend in clear words.

A slower version: Link REIT

Not every trap is dramatic. Link REIT, Asia's largest REIT and long seen as a safe income stock, reported a 6.9% drop in distribution per unit for the year to March 2026, after negative rental reversions in its Hong Kong retail portfolio (rents on renewed leases about 6.4% below the old level in the half-year to September 2025). Management expected negative reversions to continue into 2026/27. It isn't a collapse, but it's the typical pattern of a "safe" yield that shrinks year by year. A 6% yield that falls 5 to 7% a year is worth much less than it looks.

Six warning signs worth checking before buying

  1. Payout above 100% of the company's own cash measure. Distributable earnings for mortgage REITs, AFFO for REITs, free cash flow for everyone else. One quarter can be noise; three quarters is a pattern.
  2. A double-digit yield in a business that is supposed to be stable. For a REIT or utility, a yield of 10 to 16% means the market is betting on a cut. (For a cyclical shipping company with a variable payout formula, high yields are normal in strong markets; that's a different situation.)
  3. Dividend growth suddenly stops. Both Arbor and MPT had long histories of small annual raises. When the raises stopped, the board was already protecting cash.
  4. Rising leverage or refinancing pressure. When debt matures in a higher-rate environment, interest costs compete directly with the dividend.
  5. Concentration. A few tenants, borrowers or customers responsible for a large share of income.
  6. Management language changes. From "the dividend is safe" to "we're evaluating all options" or "everything is on the table". Earnings call transcripts are where this shift shows up first.

Lessons from these two cases

  • A stable-dividend stock that fails sign 1 or 2 deserves a hard look, no matter how good the story sounds.
  • Position size is the real protection. Both of these positions were small relative to my portfolio, which is why the damage stayed limited.
  • Writing down why you bought makes it easier to notice when that reason disappears, for example when the payout ratio goes above 100%.

As of today I still hold both positions; any change will show up on my portfolio page. The broader point: dividends compound only when they are covered by real cash flow.

Frequently asked questions

What is a dividend trap?

A stock whose high yield looks attractive but is high because the market expects the dividend to be cut or the business to shrink. When the cut comes, you lose income and usually part of your capital too.

Is a yield above 10% always a warning sign?

Not always. Cyclical companies with variable payout policies, such as tanker owners, can yield above 10% in strong markets by design. For businesses that are supposed to pay stable dividends, such as REITs and mortgage REITs, a double-digit yield usually means the market doubts the payout.

What is the best single indicator of a dividend cut?

In my experience the payout ratio on the cash measure the company itself uses: distributable earnings for mortgage REITs, adjusted funds from operations for REITs, free cash flow for most other companies. If it stays above 100%, the dividend is being paid from somewhere other than earnings.
