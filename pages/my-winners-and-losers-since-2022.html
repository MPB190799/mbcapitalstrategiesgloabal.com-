My Winners and Losers Since 2022: What 41 Real Positions Show

November 26, 2026 · By Marco Bozem, private investor · Not financial advice

A portfolio page usually shows the current holdings. It rarely shows which decisions worked and which didn't. Here is that view for my portfolio: all 39 stock positions worth more than about €640 each on 26 November 2026, plus my two largest losers, which have shrunk below that size. Together they are worth about €50,400, some 42% of my €120,700 portfolio; the rest is spread over many smaller positions and cash.

How to read the numbers
"Change" compares today's market value with the remaining purchase cost of the shares I hold. It is price change only, without dividends. Where I hold a stock at two brokers, the positions are combined. Source: my broker records in Parqet, 26 November 2026.

What the ranking shows

  • All ten winners are shipping or energy companies: six shipping and four oil, gas, drilling or refining. They are the core of my strategy, and in the past two years they had very strong markets behind them, especially tankers.
  • Three of the six losers are REITs, including my two worst positions. Both of the worst ones were high-yield stocks that later cut their dividends. I describe both cases in detail in dividend traps I didn't avoid.
  • Shipping is not automatically a winner. FLEX LNG is down 13% and Hapag-Lloyd about 5%, even though both pay dividends. The segment matters: crude and product tankers had an exceptional market, LNG carriers did not, and container shipping was mixed (ZIM up, Hapag-Lloyd down).
  • The winners were mostly small positions. Okeanis cost €203, Frontline €463. Big percentage gains on small amounts help less than they look; the largest gains in euros came from CMB.Tech (+€2,019), BW LPG (+€1,702), Dorian LPG (+€1,659) and TORM (+€1,559).

What I'd take from it

The pattern is consistent with how cyclical industries behave: the same sector that produced my best results can turn when its market turns. The REIT losses show the opposite risk, a seemingly stable payer whose business deteriorated. Neither result is a prediction. Both are reasons to keep position sizes limited and to check whether a dividend is covered by cash flow, which is the checklist in my research methodology.
