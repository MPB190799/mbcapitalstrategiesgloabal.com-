How International Investors
Get Taxed on Shipping Dividends

📢 Disclosure: Educational information only — not tax or investment advice. Tax rules change and depend on your personal situation; confirm with a qualified professional. This article may contain affiliate links (details).

Published: July 30, 2026 · By Marco Bozem

Shipping is the sector where my portfolio's dividend tax question basically disappears — and where a completely different, less-discussed tax question quietly takes its place. Tanker and LNG carrier owners are almost never incorporated where you'd expect, and that one fact determines both a real tax advantage and a real risk retail investors rarely check.

🚢 Shipping Hub💶 Withholding Tax Map
Short answer

Most large tanker and LNG carrier owners are incorporated in the Marshall Islands, Bermuda or Cyprus — all three of which withhold 0% on dividends and interest paid to non-residents, per PwC's Worldwide Tax Summaries for each jurisdiction. That is a genuine structural edge over most onshore energy or industrial dividends. The real tax question in shipping isn't withholding — it's whether the company could be classified as a Passive Foreign Investment Company (PFIC), a risk every major shipping company addresses directly in its own SEC filings and that carries materially worse US tax treatment if it applies.

1 · Why shipping domiciles look nothing like the flag on the ship

Every other sector I cover, the tax domicile roughly matches where the business operates. Shipping breaks that pattern completely: a tanker owner can be headquartered in Oslo or Athens, run vessels calling on ports worldwide, list its shares in New York — and be legally incorporated in a jurisdiction that has essentially nothing to do with where the ships sail or where the shares trade. That incorporation choice, not the flag on the hull or the exchange ticker, is what determines the dividend's tax treatment.

This isn't a loophole unique to a few operators — it's close to industry-standard structure. FLEX LNG is incorporated in Bermuda; Golar LNG is headquartered in Hamilton, Bermuda; Dorian LPG is a Marshall Islands company; and Frontline re-domiciled from Bermuda to Cyprus in 2022–2023, a move its own shareholders approved. The common thread across all of them: none of these jurisdictions is where the vessels operate or where most of the shares trade.

Bermuda's position is explicit in PwC's own summary: Bermuda imposes no withholding tax on any payments, including dividends, interest or capital gains, to residents or non-residents. Cyprus is equally direct — PwC's Cyprus Tax Facts confirm no withholding tax on dividends or interest paid to non-residents under domestic law, individual or corporate. The Marshall Islands' zero-withholding treatment for non-resident payments is confirmed across specialist offshore-jurisdiction tax guides referencing its corporate law framework. Compare that to the 15–25% starting points most onshore dividend domiciles carry (see the full country map linked below), and shipping's tax structure is, at the withholding level, close to the best case available.

3 · PFIC — the real tax question in shipping

Here's the part that gets skipped in most retail shipping-stock write-ups, because 0% withholding sounds like the whole story. It isn't. US tax law classifies a foreign corporation as a Passive Foreign Investment Company (PFIC) if it fails either of two annual tests: a 75% passive-income test or a 50% passive-asset test. The reason this is a live question for shipping specifically: chartering out a vessel — collecting a fixed daily rate for the use of a ship, structurally similar to renting out equipment — can look like passive "rental" income under a literal reading of the rules, even though operating a fleet of tankers is obviously an active, capital-intensive business in substance.

This is not a theoretical risk that only tax lawyers worry about — it shows up directly in shipping companies' own securities filings. Contract language addressing PFIC status and requiring companies to support US shareholders' QEF (Qualified Electing Fund) elections if PFIC status is ever triggered appears in disclosures from operators including Performance Shipping and Seanergy Maritime. A QEF election, once available, generally preserves capital-gain treatment on eventual sale and avoids the harshest "interest-charge" regime that otherwise applies to unclassified PFIC gains — but only if the company actually provides the required PFIC Annual Information Statement, and only if the US holder makes the election on time.

What I actually check: whether a shipping company's own annual report discusses its PFIC position and, if relevant, whether it commits to providing PFIC Annual Information Statements. That single disclosure line tells you more about your real US tax exposure in a shipping stock than the withholding-tax domicile does — and it's genuinely easy to miss because it's usually buried deep in the risk-factors section, not the tax-summary section.

4 · Section 883 — a different exemption, often confused with PFIC

Worth separating clearly, because the two get conflated: Section 883 of the US tax code is a different question from PFIC status. Section 883 exempts a foreign corporation's US-source income from the international operation of ships from US federal income tax — but only for companies organized in a "qualified foreign country," meaning one that has a reciprocal shipping-income tax exemption arrangement with the US. The Marshall Islands qualifies under this framework via an exchange of diplomatic notes with the US. A company also has to pass a "qualified shareholder" ownership test — broadly, that its stock is ultimately traced to individuals in qualifying countries, not opaque bearer-share structures.

Section 883 addresses whether the company itself owes US corporate tax on its US-source shipping income. PFIC status addresses whether you, as a US shareholder, get adverse treatment on your gains and distributions from owning the stock. A company can be fully compliant and exempt under Section 883 and still be a PFIC risk for its shareholders — they are separate tests answering separate questions, and a shipping company's own filings usually discuss both.

5 · What this means in practice

My practical rule: the 0% withholding at the Marshall Islands/Bermuda/Cyprus level is a real, structural edge that shows up directly in a higher net dividend versus a 15–25%-withheld onshore name paying the same headline yield. But I don't stop at the domicile map — I check whether a company's own risk factors flag active PFIC monitoring, because that's the tax exposure that can actually change my after-tax return, not the one that shows up on the dividend statement. I run the underlying fleet economics, charter coverage and cashflow the same way for every shipping name on InvestingPro, and treat the domicile and PFIC questions as the next layer down, not a substitute for that work. 6 · FAQ

Why don't shipping companies withhold tax on dividends?

Most large tanker and LNG owners are incorporated in the Marshall Islands, Bermuda or Cyprus — jurisdictions with no withholding tax on dividends to non-residents. The tax outcome follows the legal domicile, not the exchange the shares trade on or the ships' operating routes.

What is PFIC risk and why does it matter for shipping stocks?

A PFIC is a foreign corporation failing a 75% passive-income or 50% passive-asset test. Vessel charter income can resemble passive rental income under the rules, so shipping companies address PFIC status directly in their SEC filings; if triggered, US holders face worse tax treatment unless they make a timely QEF or mark-to-market election.

What is Section 883 and how does it help shipping companies avoid US tax?

It exempts a foreign corporation's US-source international shipping income from US tax, available only to companies organized in a "qualified foreign country" (including the Marshall Islands) that pass a qualified-shareholder ownership test. It's a separate question from PFIC status and from dividend withholding.

Does 0% withholding tax mean shipping dividends are tax-free for US investors?

No — it only means nothing is deducted at the foreign source. The dividend is still fully taxable ordinary income on your US return, same as any other dividend.
