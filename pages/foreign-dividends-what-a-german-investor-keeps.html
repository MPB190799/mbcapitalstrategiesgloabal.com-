What I Actually Keep From Foreign Dividends: My Real 2026 Numbers as a German Investor

November 26, 2026 · By Marco Bozem, private investor · Not financial advice

Most withholding-tax articles show you a table of statutory rates. That's useful, but it isn't what lands in your account. So here is the other side: what my broker statements actually show for 2026, company by company, as a private investor living in Germany. The data comes from my own portfolio tracking (Parqet, three personal accounts at Scalable Capital, Trade Republic and CapTrader), covering 1 January to 26 November 2026.

The short version
Gross dividends: €6,083 · Taxes withheld (foreign + German): €1,586 · Fees: €7 · Net in my account: €4,490.
That is 73.8% kept and 26.1% paid in tax, almost exactly the German flat rate. Foreign withholding cost me surprisingly little in total, because a large part of my income comes from shipping companies domiciled where no withholding tax applies.

The German baseline

For a private investor in Germany, dividends are taxed with the flat Abgeltungsteuer of 25% plus a 5.5% solidarity surcharge on that tax, which makes 26.375%. Church tax comes on top for church members. Every person also has an annual tax-free allowance (Sparer-Pauschbetrag) of €1,000 on all investment income combined.

Foreign withholding tax is credited against the German tax, but as a rule only up to the rate in the double-tax treaty, usually 15%. Anything the source country takes above that is lost unless you reclaim it there. This one rule explains most of the differences in the table below.

Pattern 1: zero-withholding domiciles land at the German rate or below

FLEX LNG (Bermuda), Dorian LPG and International Seaways (Marshall Islands), Frontline (Cyprus), BW LPG (Singapore) and TORM (a UK plc) all came in between roughly 21% and 25%. None of these jurisdictions takes withholding tax from my dividends, so the only tax is the German one, and the allowance pulled some payments below 26.375%.

This is the single biggest reason my overall rate is so close to the domestic rate even though most of my income is foreign. It's also one of the less discussed advantages of shipping stocks for European investors: the flag and the domicile are often chosen for tax and regulatory reasons that happen to suit foreign shareholders too. Pattern 2: high-withholding countries cost real money unless you reclaim

CMB.Tech is the clearest example. Belgium withholds 30% at source. Germany credits only 15%, so my German tax is reduced by 15 points and the other 15 points are simply gone, unless I file a reclaim with the Belgian tax authority. Result: 42.5% of my gross CMB.Tech dividend went to tax. On €407 that's about €60 more than the same dividend from a Marshall Islands company would have cost me.

The Norwegian (Wallenius Wilhelmsen, 25% at source) and Danish (Scandinavian Tobacco, 27% at source) lines show the same effect at around 35%. Canada (Cardinal Energy) sits at 32%: the domestic rate is 25% and the treaty rate 15%; the numbers suggest the reduced treaty rate wasn't applied at source on these payments.

Statutory withholding vs. what Germany credits (general rules for private investors; treaty details and procedures vary)
  • Belgium: 30% withheld, 15% credited, 15 points reclaimable in Belgium
  • Switzerland: 35% withheld, 15% credited, 20 points reclaimable
  • Denmark: 27% withheld, 15% credited, 12 points reclaimable
  • Norway: 25% withheld, 15% credited, 10 points reclaimable
  • United States: 15% with a W-8BEN on file at the broker, fully creditable
  • United Kingdom, Marshall Islands, Bermuda, Singapore: no withholding on dividends
  • Brazil: new 10% withholding on dividends to non-residents since 1 January 2026

Pattern 3: Brazil changed the rules this year

For decades Brazilian dividends (unlike "interest on equity", which carries 15%) reached foreign shareholders without withholding tax. Brazil's Law 15,270/2025 introduced a 10% withholding tax on dividends paid to non-residents from 1 January 2026. My Vale line at 9.8% is that new 10% almost exactly; on those payments no German tax was due because of the allowance. Later in the year, the German tax applies on top, minus the credited 10%.

If you hold Petrobras or Vale and haven't looked at your statements this year, it's worth a check. It's a small change per payment, but it's permanent.

What this means in practice

  • Withholding tax is a cost line, like a fee. A 9% gross yield from Belgium can net out lower than an 8% gross yield from a Marshall Islands company. Compare net yields, not headline yields.
  • Reclaims only pay off above a certain size. At my position sizes, the Belgian excess is about €60 a year, which has to be weighed against the paperwork. With larger positions, a reclaim clearly pays off.
  • Check the domicile, not the listing. CMB.Tech trades in New York and Brussels, TORM in New York and Copenhagen. What matters for withholding is where the company is domiciled, which you can usually see from the first two letters of the ISIN (BE, GB, MH, BM and so on).
  • The allowance is gone early in the year. The €1,000 allowance is used up by the first dividends of the year, so from spring on every euro is taxed. That's why "my effective rate is 20%" claims from January statements are misleading.

Bottom line

In 2026 I kept 73.8% of my gross dividends. The German flat tax is the main cost and unavoidable; foreign withholding was a minor factor overall because of my shipping-heavy portfolio, but a major one for individual Belgian, Nordic and Canadian holdings. If you're a European dividend investor, one look at the ISIN country code before buying saves you more than most broker comparisons.

Frequently asked questions

How much tax does a German investor pay on foreign dividends?

The German flat tax on investment income is 25% plus a 5.5% solidarity surcharge on that tax, 26.375% in total (church tax comes on top for church members). Foreign withholding tax is usually credited only up to the treaty rate, typically 15%. In my own 2026 data, total taxes came to 26.1% of gross dividends.

Which countries are expensive for German dividend investors?

Countries whose withholding tax is above the 15% that Germany credits: for example Belgium (30%), Denmark (27%), Norway (25%), Switzerland (35%) and Canada (25% if the treaty rate is not applied at source). The excess over 15% is only recovered if you file a reclaim in the source country.

Why are many shipping companies tax-efficient for foreign shareholders?

Many are domiciled in places that levy no withholding tax on dividends to foreign shareholders, such as the Marshall Islands or Bermuda. Then only your home-country tax applies. In my data, those holdings landed at roughly 21 to 23% total tax.
