Yield on Cost vs. Current Yield: What My 11 Shipping Stocks Really Paid in 2026

November 26, 2026 · By Marco Bozem, private investor · Not financial advice

Shipping is the largest theme in my portfolio. Eleven shipping companies together are worth about €22,600, roughly 19% of everything I own. In 2026 they paid me €2,054 in gross dividends. This article shows what that means position by position, and why one popular metric, yield on cost, makes these holdings look better than they really are for anyone deciding today.

The short version
Measured against what I paid, my shipping stocks yielded 17.4% in 2026. Measured against what they are worth today, they yielded 9.1%. Both numbers are correct. Only the second one tells you what the same money would earn if it were invested in these shares today.

The data

Gross dividends are from 1 January to 26 November 2026, before any tax. Cost is the remaining purchase cost of the shares I hold now; value is the market value on 26 November 2026. Why yield on cost flatters

Yield on cost rewards two things that happened in the past: buying cheaply and a rising share price. Okeanis is the extreme case. I paid €203 for my ten shares; they are now worth €740. The €75 of dividends looks like a 37% yield on what I paid. But the €740 that is invested today earns 10.2%, which is good, not spectacular.

The same pattern runs through the whole table. My shipping positions are worth about 92% more than I paid for them, so the yield on cost is roughly twice the yield on value. The yield on cost says something about my entry points. It says nothing about whether the money should stay where it is.

The exceptions are instructive

  • FLEX LNG is the only position where yield on value (11.1%) is higher than yield on cost (9.7%), because it trades below my purchase price. It has paid well while the share price went down; the total result still depends on both.
  • Dorian LPG is my largest single shipping position but has the lowest yield of the tanker and gas group at 5.5% on value. Its share price nearly doubled from my entry, and its dividends vary with LPG freight rates.
  • ZIM paid little in 2026 (2.7% on value). Its dividend is also tied to profits, and container shipping has been far weaker than tankers.

What these numbers can't tell you

Almost every company in this table pays a variable dividend tied to quarterly profit (see how the payout formulas work). 2026 was a very strong year for tanker rates, partly because of the disruption of oil flows through the Strait of Hormuz. A 9 to 11% yield on value in such a year is not a forecast for the next one; in a weak freight market the same companies can pay a fraction of this. The 2026 numbers describe a good year in a cyclical industry.

Taxes also matter: these are gross figures. As a German investor I kept roughly three quarters after tax, with large differences by country of domicile. That's covered in detail in what I actually keep from foreign dividends.

How to use this yourself

  1. Calculate both yields for your own positions. The yield-on-cost calculator does the first; for the second, divide the last twelve months of dividends by today's position value.
  2. When you decide whether to keep or add to a position, use the yield on value and the outlook for the business.
  3. Use yield on cost for what it is good at: tracking how your own income has grown relative to the money you put in.

Frequently asked questions

What is yield on cost?

Yield on cost (YOC) is the dividend you receive divided by what you originally paid for the shares. If the share price has risen since you bought, YOC is higher than the current yield that a new buyer would get.

Is a high yield on cost a reason to keep a stock?

Not on its own. The money tied up in a position is its current market value, not your purchase price. To judge whether a holding still earns its place, the current yield and the outlook for the business matter more than YOC.

Why do shipping stocks have such high yields on cost?

Many shipping companies pay out a large share of their profits, and freight rates were very strong in 2025 and 2026. Investors who bought during weaker markets now receive dividends that are large relative to their purchase price.
