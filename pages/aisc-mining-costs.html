AISC in Mining: All-In Sustaining Costs Explained

AISC (All-In Sustaining Costs) is the single most important cost metric for mining investors. It tells you how much a company spends per unit of production to keep its mines running — and therefore how profitable it really is at current commodity prices.

What Does AISC Include?

AISC was standardized by the World Gold Council in 2013 to give investors a more transparent view of mining profitability. Unlike simple cash costs, AISC captures the full picture:

  • Cash costs — direct mining, processing, refining, transport, and royalties
  • Sustaining capex — maintenance investments to keep mines operational
  • General & administrative expenses (G&A) — corporate overhead
  • Sustaining exploration — drilling and development to replace depleted reserves
  • Minus by-product credits — revenue from secondary metals (e.g. silver from a gold mine) is subtracted
Key formula: AISC = Cash Costs + Sustaining Capex + G&A + Sustaining Exploration − By-product Credits, divided by total production volume.

AISC vs. AIC: What Is the Difference?

AISC only covers costs to sustain current operations. AIC (All-In Cost) adds growth capex — new mine construction, expansions, and major development projects. For dividend investors, AISC is the more relevant metric because it shows how much free cash flow is available after maintaining production. AIC matters more when evaluating growth-stage miners building new projects.

Typical AISC Values

AISC varies significantly by commodity and producer quality:

  • Gold miners: $1,100–$1,600/oz (efficient: ~$1,200/oz, high-cost: ~$1,500+/oz)
  • Copper miners: $1.50–$3.00/lb depending on ore grade and by-product credits
  • Silver miners: $14–$22/oz for primary silver producers

Why AISC Matters for Dividend Investors

The AISC margin (commodity price minus AISC) directly drives free cash flow, which funds dividends and buybacks. A gold miner with AISC of $1,300/oz and a gold price of $2,800/oz has a $1,500/oz margin — generating enormous cash flow. When AISC rises faster than commodity prices, margins compress and dividends are at risk.

Always compare AISC across companies in the same commodity space. A low-AISC producer is structurally more resilient in downturns and can maintain dividends when higher-cost competitors are forced to cut.

How to Use AISC When Comparing Mining Stocks

When evaluating two gold miners, start with AISC. Barrick Gold (2025 AISC: ~$1,320/oz) versus Newmont (AISC: ~$1,475/oz) — at a gold price of $3,000/oz, Barrick generates ~$1,680/oz in gross margin versus Newmont's ~$1,525/oz. Over millions of ounces, that difference compounds into meaningfully higher free cash flow per share. B2Gold, one of the lower-cost operators at ~$1,050/oz AISC, has among the widest margins in the sector.

Watch for AISC inflation: labor costs, fuel prices, and ore grade decline can all push AISC higher. A miner with 5% annual AISC inflation at $1,300/oz base reaches $1,650+/oz within five years — significantly compressing margins if gold prices stagnate. This is why I weight AISC trajectory as heavily as the current level.

AISC in Copper Mining: A Different Dynamic

Copper AISC is measured per pound of copper (lb), and by-product credits from gold, silver, and molybdenum significantly influence net costs. Diversified miners like BHP and Rio Tinto have low net copper AISC partly because of significant by-product silver and molybdenum revenue. Pure-play copper miners at high-altitude operations (e.g. Andes region) face higher energy and logistics costs but may access higher-grade ore. Check if AISC is reported as "co-product" vs "by-product" — the accounting method dramatically changes the stated number.

AISC and Dividend Sustainability in Mining

For dividend investors, AISC is directly linked to dividend safety. Mining companies with a large margin between the commodity price and their AISC can maintain — and often grow — their dividends through commodity price downturns. Those operating near their AISC are one bad quarter away from a cut.

Most dividend-paying miners use one of two structures:

  • Fixed-plus-variable dividends: A base dividend set conservatively (often at 40–50% of FCF at median commodity prices) plus a variable top-up when margins expand. Agnico Eagle, B2Gold, and many mid-tier gold miners use this model. The base is protected; the variable disappears when margins compress.
  • Percentage-of-FCF payout: The company commits to paying out a defined percentage (often 50–75%) of free cash flow as dividends. This means AISC and commodity prices jointly determine how much you receive. Thungela Resources and Warrior Met Coal have operated this way in coal; several gold streamers use similar frameworks.

In both cases, AISC is the denominator. Higher AISC relative to the commodity price means less FCF available for distribution. When I screen mining companies for dividend quality, AISC margin (commodity price minus AISC) is the first filter, before looking at yield, payout ratio, or management credibility.

AISC for Silver, Uranium, and Thermal Coal

AISC is widely used in gold and increasingly standardized in other commodities, but the calculation method varies:

  • Silver: Primary silver producers report AISC per ounce of silver equivalent. The by-product credit methodology is critical here — a silver miner with significant gold and zinc by-products can report negative or near-zero net AISC while a primary silver producer with few by-products shows $18–$22/oz. Always read the footnotes on by-product assumptions.
  • Uranium: Uranium miners typically report costs per pound of U3O8. AISC-equivalent metrics vary by producer. Cameco and Kazatomprom report cash costs and unit operating costs; the industry does not yet have a universal AISC standard comparable to gold. Compare break-even costs at current spot prices ($80–$90/lb U3O8 range in 2025–2026).
  • Thermal and metallurgical coal: Coal miners report FOB mine cost per metric tonne. Thungela Resources (South Africa), Coronado Global Resources (Australia/USA), and Arch Resources operate in the $80–$130/t cost range for export coal, with revenue sensitivity to Newcastle and seaborne met coal prices. AISC-style analysis applies: wide margins fund dividends, compressed margins threaten them.
  • Copper: As noted, copper AISC is per pound and depends heavily on by-product accounting. Net copper AISC for a mine with significant molybdenum credits can look misleadingly low. Southern Copper (Grupo Mexico) reports some of the lowest net copper costs globally due to by-product revenue from silver and molybdenum.

AISC Red Flags: What to Watch in Earnings Reports

Mining companies report AISC in their quarterly results. Here are the warning signs I look for:

  1. AISC rising faster than commodity prices: If gold is up 10% year-over-year but a miner's AISC is up 12%, their margin is actually shrinking despite the higher price. This often precedes dividend guidance cuts.
  2. Guidance increases mid-year: When a miner raises its AISC guidance during the year (e.g. from $1,200 to $1,350/oz), it usually reflects operational problems — lower ore grades, equipment failures, labor disputes, or permitting delays. Treat upward AISC revisions as yellow flags.
  3. Sustaining capex cuts: Companies can temporarily suppress reported AISC by deferring sustaining capex. This improves short-term margins but accelerates mine degradation and future cost inflation. Look at sustaining capex as a percentage of revenue over time — a shrinking ratio is a warning sign.
  4. Currency tailwinds masking real cost problems: Miners operating in weaker-currency countries (South Africa, Canada, Australia) can show flat dollar-denominated AISC even while local currency costs are rising. A strengthening ZAR, CAD, or AUD will immediately expose the underlying cost pressure in dollar terms.
  5. Grade decline not offset by expansion: As mines mature, ore grades typically decline — meaning more rock must be processed per ounce of gold recovered. Rising AISC from grade decline is structural and usually cannot be reversed without drilling new higher-grade zones or acquiring new assets.

How to Build an AISC Comparison Table

When I analyze mining sector positions, I build a simple AISC comparison table for each commodity I hold. For gold (as of Q1 2026 based on latest public reports):

Gold Miners AISC Snapshot (Q1 2026, approximate):
B2Gold (BTG): ~$1,050–1,080/oz — lowest-cost tier, strong FCF margin at current gold prices
Agnico Eagle (AEM): ~$1,230–1,260/oz — first-quartile, reliable dividend grower
Barrick Gold (GOLD): ~$1,310–1,340/oz — mid-tier, improving with Nevada complex
Newmont (NEM): ~$1,450–1,480/oz — highest of the majors, recent operational challenges
Gold price (June 2026): approximately $3,000+/oz → margins are exceptional across the sector

This table immediately shows which companies have the most dividend headroom at current gold prices and which are most vulnerable to a gold price correction. If gold dropped to $2,400/oz, Newmont's margin would compress to ~$920/oz while B2Gold's would still be ~$1,350/oz. That difference compounds into very different FCF and dividend outcomes.
